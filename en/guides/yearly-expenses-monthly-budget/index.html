How to fit yearly bills into a monthly budget

There are two clean ways to handle yearly bills: either divide the yearly amount by twelve and set it aside every month (a sinking fund), or reserve the full amount in the month it's due – from the first day of that month, not just when it's charged. The only wrong way is to forget it.

Why yearly bills break a budget

Car insurance and liability cover arrive in January, vehicle tax in May, the credit card's annual fee in autumn. Each one is manageable on its own. But it lands in a month where you didn't expect it – and then there's not enough for everyday spending.

Calendar first, then the method

  1. Collect all yearly items – the fixed expenses list helps.
  2. Note the month for each. It's on the insurance policy, the tax notice or last year's statements.
  3. Mark months with several items. If two or three amounts pile up in one month, dividing by twelve is usually more relaxed.
  4. Treat quarterly items the same way: either ÷ 3 every month or the full amount in each of the four due months.

Why from the first day of the month?

If the insurance is charged on the 20th, your account looks better than it is until the 19th. Spend the money before then and it's missing on the 20th. So the amount comes out of your available money from the start of the month – no matter when it actually leaves.

With the app

Yearly fixed costs with a due date

In Simple Household Budget, setup already asks about yearly items such as liability insurance, credit card and bank card fees – each with its due date. Others, like car insurance, vehicle tax or contents insurance, can be added with the plus button. Fixed costs can be monthly, quarterly, yearly or one-off.

The app reserves the amount only in the month it's due – and from the first day of that month. If you prefer to divide by twelve, add a monthly item called “Savings pot” instead.

Questions

Do I need a separate account for a sinking fund?

Not necessarily, but it helps. Money in your current account looks available and is easily spent. A separate savings account or pot keeps it visibly apart.

What about surprise back-payments, like utility bills?

A back-payment comes once and in an unknown amount. If you're worried about it, set aside a small amount each month. When it comes, take it from the pot, not from the current month.

Is paying yearly instead of monthly worth it?

Some insurers charge less for annual payment. Then it's worth it – as long as you plan for the amount as described above.
